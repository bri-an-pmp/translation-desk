# Certified translation of official documents

Birth, marriage and death certificates, diplomas and transcripts, police records, court orders, IDs and similar documents, translated for an immigration office (such as USCIS), a court, a school, a licensing board or a consulate. The reader checks that **nothing is missing and nothing was changed**. A complete, literal and faithful translation matters more than elegance.

## Completeness

- Translate every piece of text on the document: headings, form labels, printed fine print, footers, margins, reverse side, stamps, handwritten notes, and blank fields.
- Never summarize, skip "boilerplate" or leave anything for the translator to fill in later.
- Blank fields stay visible: `Profession: [Blank]`.
- Text you can't read: `[Illegible]`, never a guess. If you can read part of it, translate that part and mark the rest: `Juan [illegible] Pérez`.

## Non-text elements

Describe them in square brackets, **in the target language**, exactly where they appear on the page. Use these forms:

## Names, numbers and dates

- **People's names** stay exactly as written, with every accent and both surnames (María Guadalupe Hernández de la Cruz). Never anglicize or translate them.
- **ID, registration, folio, book, page, certificate and CURP/RFC numbers** stay character for character. A single wrong digit can invalidate the document.
- **Numeric dates** stay in the document's format (`12/03/2024`). Don't reorder them; flag the format once so the translator can confirm (day/month/year in most Spanish-speaking countries).
- **Dates written in words** are translated in words ("doce de marzo de dos mil veinticuatro" → "the twelfth of March of two thousand twenty-four").
- **Currency amounts** stay as written.

## Places and institutions

- Countries are translated (México → Mexico, Estados Unidos → United States).
- States, cities, municipalities and towns keep their official names (Jalisco, Guadalajara, Tlaquepaque). Translate the generic word around them: "Municipio de Zapopan" → "Municipality of Zapopan"; "Estado de México" → "State of Mexico".
- Government offices and positions are translated by their function, with the original in parentheses and italics on first mention: Civil Registry (*Registro Civil*); Registry Officer (*Oficial del Registro Civil*). After that, use the translation only.
- Schools and universities keep their official names; add a translation in brackets on first mention if it helps: Universidad Autónoma de Guadalajara [Autonomous University of Guadalajara].

## Layout

- Keep the reading order of the document, top to bottom and left to right.
- Form fields become `Label: value` lines, with the label translated and the value copied or translated as appropriate.
- Tables become one line per row, with the cells separated by ` | `.
- Keep paragraph breaks, numbering and indentation markers.

## Faithfulness

- Translate errors as they are, and flag them (a misspelled name, an impossible date, a typo in an ID number). Never correct the document.
- Keep abbreviations when their meaning is uncertain, and flag them; expand only abbreviations you are sure of, keeping the original in brackets: `Lic. [Licenciado, a professional title]`.
- Don't add explanations inside the translation except the bracketed element descriptions and first-mention originals above. Put everything else in flags.
- Don't write the translator's certificate. The tool adds it when it builds the delivery file.

## Notary public

A U.S. notary public is not a *notario público*. A notary can notarize the **translator's signature** on the certificate, but a notary may never notarize their own signature: if the translator is also a notary, a different notary must notarize it. Never describe the translator as certifying anything as a notary.
